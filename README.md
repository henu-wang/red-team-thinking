# Red Team Thinking

Frameworks and techniques for applying red team thinking to improve decisions, challenge assumptions, and stress-test strategies before committing resources.

## What Is Red Team Thinking?

Red team thinking is the practice of deliberately challenging plans, strategies, and assumptions by adopting an adversarial perspective. Originally developed in military and intelligence contexts, red teaming has proven invaluable in business strategy, cybersecurity, policy-making, and personal decision-making.

The core principle is simple: before you commit to a course of action, have someone (or force yourself) to systematically attack it. Find the weaknesses before reality does. This approach counteracts the natural human tendency toward confirmation bias, groupthink, and overconfidence.

## Why Red Teaming Matters

Organizations and individuals fail not because they lack information but because they fail to challenge their own assumptions. History is filled with catastrophic failures that could have been prevented by systematic adversarial analysis:

- The Bay of Pigs invasion (1961): groupthink prevented critical challenge
- The Challenger disaster (1986): dissenting voices were suppressed
- The 2008 financial crisis: risk models were not stress-tested against adversarial scenarios
- Countless startup failures: founders refused to test their core assumptions

Red teaming is the antidote to these failures. It creates a structured, socially acceptable way to challenge ideas and surface hidden risks.

## The Red Team Framework

### Phase 1: Define the Target

Clearly identify what you are testing. This could be a business strategy, an investment thesis, a product plan, a negotiation approach, or any significant decision. Write down the core assumptions, the expected outcomes, and the logic chain connecting your actions to those outcomes.

### Phase 2: Assemble the Red Team

The ideal red team includes people who think differently from the decision-makers. Look for diversity of perspective, expertise, and cognitive style. If working alone, you must deliberately adopt an adversarial mindset -- imagine you are being paid to find flaws.

### Phase 3: Attack the Assumptions

For each core assumption, ask:
- What evidence would disprove this assumption?
- Under what conditions would this assumption fail?
- What are we taking for granted that we should not?
- What information are we ignoring or dismissing?
- Who benefits from us believing this assumption?

### Phase 4: Stress-Test the Plan

Apply adversarial scenarios to the plan:
- What if our key resource becomes unavailable?
- What if our timeline doubles?
- What if a competitor responds aggressively?
- What if our core assumption is completely wrong?
- What if the environment changes dramatically?

### Phase 5: Report and Integrate

Document all findings and integrate them into the decision-making process. The goal is not to kill the plan but to improve it. Every vulnerability identified before execution is a vulnerability that can be addressed.

## Red Team Techniques

### Technique 1: The Pre-Mortem
Imagine the project has failed spectacularly. Each team member independently writes down why it failed. This surfaces risks that social pressure normally suppresses.

### Technique 2: The Devil's Advocate
Assign someone the explicit role of arguing against the proposed course of action. Give them time, resources, and social permission to be thorough.

### Technique 3: Competitive Simulation
Have team members role-play as competitors, regulators, or other adversaries. What would they do to defeat your plan?

### Technique 4: Assumption Mapping
List every assumption underlying your plan, rate each by importance and uncertainty, then systematically test the most critical ones.

### Technique 5: The 10th Man Rule
Inspired by Israeli intelligence doctrine: if nine people in a group agree, the tenth person must actively disagree and build the strongest possible countercase, regardless of personal opinion.

### Technique 6: Alternative Analysis
Generate at least three fundamentally different explanations for the same evidence or three fundamentally different approaches to the same problem. This prevents premature convergence on a single interpretation.

## Applying Red Team Thinking to Personal Decisions

Red teaming is not just for organizations. You can apply it to personal decisions:

**Major purchases**: Before buying a house or car, spend an hour building the strongest case for not buying it.

**Career decisions**: Before accepting a job offer, list every reason why the role could go wrong.

**Investments**: Before buying a stock or fund, write the bear case in detail.

**Relationships**: Before making major commitments, honestly assess the risks and red flags you might be overlooking.

## Common Pitfalls in Red Teaming

1. **Performative red teaming**: Going through the motions without genuine adversarial effort
2. **Shooting the messenger**: Punishing the red team for finding problems
3. **Timing**: Starting too late when commitment is already locked in
4. **Scope**: Making the red team scope too narrow to find systemic issues
5. **Authority**: Not giving the red team access to real information
6. **Follow-through**: Identifying vulnerabilities but not addressing them

## Building a Red Team Culture

The most effective organizations make adversarial thinking a permanent feature rather than an occasional exercise. This requires leadership that genuinely values dissent, processes that create space for challenge, and rewards for identifying problems before they become failures.
